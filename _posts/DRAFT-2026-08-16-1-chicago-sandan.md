In all honesty, I wasn’t looking forward attending the Umu Igbo Unite conference, an annual event meant to bring together young adults of the Nigerian Igbo diaspora. The tickets were expensive and I didn’t know anyone who was going. And would I fit in with the crowds of beautiful, IG worthy Igbo women? I thought to back out, but my sister convinced me to attend and said she would go with me. She’d attended some years back and described it as a transformative (and fun) experience. The tickets were nonrefundable, but at least I’d have a buddy with me. Reluctantly, I caught yet another flight home. Since the conference was in the city, I could attend during the day and stay home at night, so I wouldn’t need to spend more money on a hotel. A win is a win, I suppose.
---
We missed all the events on the first day except the comedy show. I decided to wear a short skirt, and before I left Mom told me to bring a sweater and being the (barely) rebellious daughter that I am, I skipped out. Upon entering the Hyatt Regency at McCormick, I immediately began to regret my decision. It was cold like a hospital floor. There were signs for directions, but these signs were for a sex conference that was apparently happening at the same time as the UIU conference. A part of me wanted to go to the sex conference, but then again, I’m not a sex-haver, so it wouldn’t be relevant now.  
We arrived at the ballroom, which was filled with well-dressed Igbo men and women. They were behind schedule when we arrived. There was a UIU (the organization) trivia Kahoot that my sister was enthusiastic about, but they didn’t end up posting the final leaderboard (instead, they said, they would contact the leader directly), much to the disappointment of my sister.  What I learned: UIU had existed since the 90’s, and Phoenix was home to the newest chapter.
There was a point in the evening when the announcer called the name of each Nigerian state and asked the audience to cheer if they were from there. Of course, the loudest state was Anambra. Knowing Nigeria’s history, my sister and I wondering if this silly game was encouraging the brutal divides that seemed to endlessly hold Nigeria back. But. Maybe a game is just a game. Anyway – go Anambra!!!
Then the comedians arrived. They were enthusiastic for sure, but I didn’t understand the jokes. My sister described it as “Instagram skits in real life.” I suggested we leave early and stop by the hotel restaurant before it closed to try the fries. My sister considered, then obliged. The restaurant had a brooding lighting and was mostly empty. My sister wanted to get desert too but the waiter warned us against it (It’s too sweet, he said), so we settled on a bowl of fries to share. Contrary to the pictures online, they were mediocre - there goes $6. We headed back to the hallway outside the ballroom. The comedy show had just ended, and the crowds were clearly out. My sister was determined to talk to at least one person. We stopped a girl who had beautiful hair, a half-up-half-down style, and being as unoriginal as I am, I took a picture to show to my stylist (result below?). Before we departed, we stopped at the photobooth, where the lighting was dim and the flash was loud, snap-snap! Captured like celebrities on the red carpet!
---
Chicago has several artificial biodomes they call “beaches,” which are really concrete slabs sprinkled with sand across the Lake Michigan coastline. My sister and I arrived late afternoon for the UIU beach party, me with my sunhat and umbrella and book. We’d brought friends, one a woman we met on a whim offering a ride for carpooling, and another that my sister had met at a previous conference. Thus, I had fulfilled my friend making quota of the day for making new friends. There was jollof rice, chicken, and ice pops, a cool relief to the humid air. I sat and opened my book, Ask and it is given, assigned by my therapist many moons ago. It was repetitive novel, and occasionally insane. But there was good things to be said. Anything you desire can be yours, was the message echoed. Anything?
The beach gradually grew crowded. I could see the lifeguards in their red swimsuits, sitting in the highchairs or on tiny boats in the water, turning their heads and watching. All kinds of folks in the water, all types of bodies, in swimsuits of neon and neutral tones. The jutting gray buildings of the skyline cut the beach like a knife, and the sun bounced off the water like drops of gold. Anything I desire could be mine. What do I really want?
