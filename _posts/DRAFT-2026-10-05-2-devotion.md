_[Sometime in September]_

I understand theatre kids now, the memorization of the songs, the constant outbursts of music, cosplay, long Reddit threads, general obsession. I understand it all after seeing _Les Misérables_ at the Phoenix Theatre Company. This time, I went with Johnny and his friend Jean to the production. On the stage, I was witness to the dedication to perfection, watching the actors transition between pivotal moments through song and dance. The highlight to me was Éponine's heartache - a woman written to be destroyed by unfulfilled desire. It's not often that woman are written to lose - usually it's men we see stuck in the "friendzone." I was happy/sad to see Éponine pine endlessly, and ultimately, <details><summary> Spoilers! die without achieving romantic love. Happy because I love to see woman characters experience emotions that are relatable. Sad, because to see her demise on the stage in the arms of Marius hurt my heart, she was loved by him, but it would never be in the way she wanted - did this make her selfish, to ask for something he couldn't give? The musical always ends the same, with her broken heart. </summary></details> Poor Éponine! 

My favorite actors/actresses of the night were Tre Moore as Marius, whose carefully portrayed the dueling love he shared for Cosette and Éponine. Also, Ethan Rogers as Jean Valjean, who looked good... but also portrayed Valjean with the humility and courage that is present in his character.

I haven't seen a musical since high school maybe, and I was nervous I wouldn't know what was going on, but I kept up just fine. The orchestra, barely visible under the stage deck, was immersive and cascading, and even though I couldn't understand every lyric, the emotions of each scene were easy to discern. I probably should not have gotten up at 4am that day though!

After seeing Les Misérables, I stopped by my house and then headed to Glendale for Maya's pole dance recital. Like the production of Les Misérables, the recital laid out a stage and a cast of characters using their bodies to tell a story. And you could see from their movements the level of devotion required to twist and turn through the complex choreographies created for the performance. There was one woman, a mother of many talents, who glowed under the lights and moved with precision, but as she motioned to remove her overcoat, her zipper got stuck, and she ended up starting the routine over. For a moment, she looked shaken, maybe annoyed at how quickly the dance she had crafted was derailed. But when the music restarted, she snapped back into focus, and this time, how overcoat slipped off with ease, as if thrusted by the wind. 

When Maya performed, I again could understand the lure of the pole. The way you could manipulate physics, bend bodies, build momentum, and propel yourself - I could tell, although her routine was carefully put together, there was a sense a freedom evoked, no bashfulness, no shyness, no hiding, just a real body in motion. Maya's routine was one only she could do, a fusion of Jamaican and Black culture, along with her own sense of style. There were elements of her dancing that I'd seen glimpses of when we'd went out to clubs, and gravity defying motions on the pole, when she pushed herself upwards and contorted in a way that defied gravity. The work that she had put in, for many months, all came together that night, and yet, she made it look effortless. And that is the art of devotion.

Afterwards, Maya, Lanae, Joyce, and I took a group picture and headed out to _Thirsty Lion_ for a celebratory dinner. Filling my car was a song I had been listening to some years ago, that had randomly come to mind: _get out of my head._
